The Guardian view on the digital pound: an impetuous idea with a risky momentum of its own

Two years ago, the House of Lords looked into whether the Bank of England should issue digital pounds to be held in electronic wallets. Peers were unconvinced, asking if this wasn't "a solution in search of a problem". Last December, MPs on the Treasury select committee said the same thing. The government's response, however, was to tell the public to prepare for a "Britcoin" to be in use by the end of the decade.

Physical money has been around for thousands of years for good reason. Cash is convenient, reliable and anonymous. It seems unlikely that these advantages could be replicated online. State-backed e-cash might look inevitable to officials who say money is already digital, pointing to cryptocurrencies and smartphone payments. But central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) are different. A Britcoin would shift the management burden from commercial banks to Threadneedle Street.

Paul Tucker, a former deputy Bank governor, argued in his book Global Discord that the West is responding to China's digital renminbi, which potentially gives the state social control through direct access to banking transactions. Yet the UK government can already spy on commercial bank accounts. It doesn't do so without good reason. Brett Scott, a former broker and the author of Cloudmoney, accepts that there is a geopolitical rivalry. The difference, he says, is that private corporations can push their agendas harder in the West than in Beijing.

During the pandemic, finance and technology firms accelerated moves towards a cashless society, which saw hitherto unimaginable levels of surveillance and data extraction. A world without notes and coins would hurt those least able to afford it. Poor and elderly people often prefer to budget with cash. A million UK adults don't have a bank account. The Royal Society of Arts calculated that going cash-free risked 10 million Britons losing control of their finances. The prospect of a cashless society also unnerved central banks. They worried about being dominated by commercial banks. Their answer: central banks should have their own digital currencies.

However, if central banks pushed CBDCs, they risked destabilising the financial institutions they oversaw. Central banks can't go bankrupt. People would vote with their feet if they could effectively open accounts at the Bank of England. According to a 2019 survey of 23 central banks, 82% of respondents worried that digital currencies could cause runs on banks. To address concerns, caps on CBDC holdings have been suggested. But limits may put off many from using digital pounds.

Mr Scott rightly compares the digitalisation of money to Uberisation — a process where convenience is traded for societal threats that older behaviours kept in check. That's why we need a debate rather than passively accepting state-backed digital money. Facebook's global cryptocurrency, Diem, was killed off by money-laundering concerns. But pervasive monitoring of spending changes society's psychology for the worse.

Right-wing conspiracy theories have been launched by excitable talk about the potential to place conditions on who can use digital money, for what, and with whom. Technically feasible? Eventually. Politically realisable? Gradually. Desirable? Well, that depends on your perspective. Maybe in developing countries, a digital central bank currency could be used — as was attempted briefly in Ecuador — to stymie an overly powerful, and US-aligned, domestic finance sector. But for the UK, it is an idea whose time has yet to come.

Source: The Guardian, "The Guardian view on the digital pound" — Editorial, 12 February 2024.

Step 1 / 7

Understand

Before you can summarise a text, you need to separate two things that students often confuse: what the text is about, and what the author thinks about it.

Topic

The subject the text discusses. Neutral — it doesn't tell you the author's opinion.

Author's position

What the author actually argues or concludes about that topic.

TOPIC ≠ AUTHOR'S POSITION — a good summary always states both, separately.
What is the TOPIC of this article?

Step 2 / 7

Select

Not every sentence deserves a place in your summary. Learn to recognise a main idea — and tell it apart from an example, a statistic, or a claim that is too vague to be useful.

2.1 — Spot the main idea

This passage from the text can be rewritten four ways. Which formulation actually states its main idea?

Same task, a different passage.
"According to a 2019 survey of 23 central banks, 82% of respondents worried that digital currencies could cause runs on banks. To address concerns, caps on CBDC holdings have been suggested."

2.2 — Keep or leave out?

For each statement drawn from the article, decide: does it belong in a summary?

MAIN IDEAS → keep. EXAMPLES, FIGURES and SECONDARY SOURCES → usually leave out.

3.3 — Find the right level of detail

Match each candidate sentence about cash's advantages to the right label.

"Cash is convenient because it works in shops and markets, never runs out of battery, and can't be traced or hacked, unlike apps."
"Cash has practical advantages that are convenient, reliable and anonymous — qualities that are genuinely hard to reproduce in a fully digital system."
"Cash also works during power cuts or internet outages, and gives users an exact daily record they can carry physically in their wallet."
Step 4 / 7

Reformulate

Four reusable techniques to put ideas into your own words — the skill you'll need for every future summary, not just this one.

4.1 — Synonym

Replace harm with a suitable synonym and rewrite the sentence.
"A cashless society would harm those least able to afford it."
Model — A cashless society would disadvantage those least able to afford it.

4.2 — Change the structure

Rewrite this sentence in the passive voice.
"The government is promoting a digital pound."
Model — A digital pound is being promoted by the government.

4.3 — Generalise

These three groups share something in common. Name the category in a few words.
poor people · elderly people · people without a bank account
Model — vulnerable groups

4.4 — Combine

Merge these three short sentences into one.
"Cash is convenient. Cash is reliable. Cash is anonymous."
Model — Cash offers practical advantages and greater privacy.

Model answer

The article examines whether the Bank of England should introduce a digital pound, or Britcoin. The government wants to launch it by the end of the decade, although the House of Lords and the Treasury select committee both doubted its usefulness, calling it a solution without a real problem. The author argues that cash still offers real advantages: it is convenient, reliable and anonymous, qualities hard to reproduce online. It also points out that, unlike ordinary digital payments, a central bank digital currency would transfer part of the management of money from commercial banks to the central bank, partly as a response to China's digital renminbi. Another concern is that a cashless society could exclude vulnerable groups, such as poor, elderly or unbanked people, and increase surveillance of everyday spending. Central banks themselves fear that such currencies could trigger bank runs by encouraging people to move savings away from commercial banks. Finally, the article concludes that the digital pound is a premature idea and that a genuine public debate is needed before it is adopted.

Summary Method

  1. Understand

    What is the text about? What is the author's position?

  2. Select

    Keep main ideas. Remove unnecessary evidence.

  3. Condense

    Combine several details into one broader idea.

  4. Reformulate

    Use your own words and structures.

  5. Organise

    Build a logical plan.

  6. Write

    Be concise, accurate and neutral.

  7. Check

    Content, selection, reformulation, organisation, language and word count.
