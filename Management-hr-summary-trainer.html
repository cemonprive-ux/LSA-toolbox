Workers prioritising employers that offer sabbaticals in work-life balance shift

Rachel Hall, The Guardian, August 27, 2024

More than half of managers (53%) said their organisation offered sabbatical leave, with just over a quarter (29%) saying their organisation did not, according to exclusive data from the Chartered Management Institute. Managers working in the public or charity sector were more likely to say sabbatical leave was available than those in the private sector (62% v 44%).

Sabbaticals were popular with employees and employers because they offered benefits for both, said Anthony Painter, the director of policy at the Chartered Management Institute. "In today's competitive job market, companies are doubling their efforts to boost staff retention," he said. "They can hit the sweet spot between employee wellbeing and upskilling that benefits the employer. The relationship between employer and employee has shifted in recent years but it's not zero-sum."

The survey also found that younger managers, under 55, were more likely to say that it was important for employers to offer sabbatical leave (80% compared with 72%).

Two-fifths of the managers said the main reason for offering sabbatical leave was to improve employee wellbeing and mental health, while 36% wanted to cultivate a more flexible work culture and increase talent retention (31%).

Recent Harvard Business Review research stated that sabbaticals were growing exponentially, while Adecco, the world's largest HR company, identified them as one of the top five workplace trends.

Shasa Dobrow, the associate professor of management at the London School of Economics, said there had been "big shifts in how we understand work and where it fits in our lives" in recent years. This has meant that people are less likely to prioritise salary, and instead focus on "what is meaningful to them" (…). "Organisations that offer attractive sabbatical options will be clearly signalling to both prospective and current employees that they value employees' wellbeing and access to important developmental opportunities," she said.

Sabbaticals are appealing to employers as they boost job satisfaction levels and reduce turnover. She said her research had found that as people progressed in their job, their satisfaction tended to decrease. When they switched jobs and started in a new organisation, it rose, before declining again. "What's interesting is that there are ways to mimic this effect without actually changing organisations — or even jobs — and that is through doing things like sabbaticals. We think it's the spark from the novelty and change that comes from activities like sabbaticals that can help people experience a boost in their job satisfaction," she said.

Alan Greef, an NHS theatre service manager at County hospital Louth, said his hospital had been running sabbaticals since 1952 when they were introduced to improve employee retention and wellbeing (…).

Al Mayeeda Kinnory, an HR consultant, said in her experience about 15-25% of employees took advantage of sabbatical leave when it was offered, though it depended on the industry and company culture, with uptake higher in progressive sectors such as tech or creative industries. The organisations she consults for usually offer three- to six-month sabbaticals, available to employees after five to 10 years of continuous service. In some cases, they are paid, potentially partially, in others unpaid, and this can depend on the purpose of the leave, for example whether it involves upskilling or community work.

She said this reflected the fact that HR strategies were increasingly aimed at fostering a "supportive and progressive workplace culture that values and prioritises employee wellbeing and work-life balance". Employees often came back with "renewed energy and a fresh perspective which also helps to revitalise the mood for the further work," she said (…).

Step 1 / 7

Understand

Guiding question: What managerial challenges will companies face in a rapidly evolving world of work? Before you can summarise a text, separate what it is about from what the author thinks about it.

Topic

The subject the text discusses. Neutral — it doesn't tell you the author's opinion.

Author's position

What the author actually argues or concludes about that topic.

TOPIC ≠ AUTHOR'S POSITION — a good summary always states both, separately.

Step 2 / 7

Select

Not every sentence deserves a place in your summary. Learn to recognise a main idea — and tell it apart from an example, a statistic, or a claim that is too vague to be useful.

2.1 — Spot the main idea

This passage from the text can be rewritten four ways. Which formulation actually states its main idea?
"More than half of managers (53%) said their organisation offered sabbatical leave... Managers working in the public or charity sector were more likely to say sabbatical leave was available than those in the private sector (62% v 44%)."
Same task, a different passage.
"...her research had found that as people progressed in their job, their satisfaction tended to decrease. When they switched jobs... it rose... 'there are ways to mimic this effect... through doing things like sabbaticals... the spark from the novelty and change...'"

2.2 — Keep or leave out?

For each statement drawn from the article, decide: does it belong in a summary?

MAIN IDEAS → keep. EXAMPLES, FIGURES and SECONDARY SOURCES → usually leave out.

Step 4 / 7

Reformulate

Four reusable techniques to put ideas into your own words — the skill you'll need for every future summary, not just this one.

4.1 — Synonym

Replace boost with a suitable synonym and rewrite the sentence.
"Sabbaticals boost job satisfaction levels and reduce turnover."
Model — Sabbaticals increase job satisfaction levels and reduce turnover.

4.2 — Change the structure

Rewrite this sentence in the passive voice.
"The Chartered Management Institute collected exclusive data on sabbatical leave."
Model — Exclusive data on sabbatical leave was collected by the Chartered Management Institute.

4.3 — Generalise

These four things share something in common. Name the category in a few words.
wellbeing · mental health · flexible culture · talent retention
Model — employee-focused benefits

4.4 — Combine

Merge these three short sentences into one.
"Sabbaticals improve employee wellbeing. Sabbaticals increase flexibility. Sabbaticals boost staff retention."
Model — Sabbaticals improve employee wellbeing, increase flexibility and boost staff retention.

Model answer

The article examines a survey by the Chartered Management Institute on the growing use of sabbatical leave in the workplace. The author explains that sabbatical leave is now offered by a majority of organisations, and is more common in the public and charity sector than in the private sector. It also points out that employers offer sabbaticals mainly to improve employee wellbeing, encourage flexibility and increase staff retention, in a context where workers increasingly value meaning over salary. Another point is that sabbaticals can artificially recreate the boost in job satisfaction that normally comes from changing jobs, without employees actually having to leave their organisation. The article notes that uptake and practical terms vary widely between industries, particularly in progressive sectors such as tech. Finally, the article concludes that sabbaticals reflect a wider shift towards HR strategies that prioritise employee wellbeing and work-life balance.

Summary Method

  1. Understand

    What is the text about? What is the author's position?

  2. Select

    Keep main ideas. Remove unnecessary evidence.

  3. Condense

    Combine several details into one broader idea.

  4. Reformulate

    Use your own words and structures.

  5. Organise

    Build a logical plan.

  6. Write

    Be concise, accurate and neutral.

  7. Check

    Content, selection, reformulation, organisation, language and word count.
