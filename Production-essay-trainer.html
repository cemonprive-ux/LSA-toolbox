Tariffs and Global Supply Chains

Cette page prépare une réponse ORALE structurée à la question posée sur le texte, pas une dissertation écrite. Vous apprenez une méthode transférable : analyser la question, construire des arguments avec le texte ET vos connaissances, préparer des notes courtes, puis parler à partir de ces notes.

Discussion question
How do tariffs affect global supply chains?
Source Document

Who pays for Trump's tariffs? Depends on the product and the country.

Since taking office, President Donald Trump has threatened tariffs on an array of trading partners, including Mexico, Canada, Europe, Colombia and India. He has instituted a 10 percent tariff on Chinese goods and a 25 percent tariff on aluminum and steel imports.

Which nation ultimately bears the cost of a tariff depends on what that country makes and its pull in the global market. But economists warn that the added tax will largely fall on American businesses and consumers.

The United States is the largest importer of goods in the world, giving it significant sway in a trade war. It is also the top export market for more than 20 countries, making exports to the United States a sizable portion of many countries' economies.

Mexico's and Canada's economies are the most reliant on exports to the United States; both countries were threatened with 25 percent tariffs that are now under a 30-day reprieve. But that doesn't mean they are necessarily the most vulnerable to tariffs. Who bears the cost and risk of the trade tax depends more on the product than where it is made.

A country is most vulnerable if it primarily produces goods that can easily be made in many places, such as garments or agricultural products, and the United States is its biggest customer. In those cases, American importers could substitute goods from another country to avoid paying the tariff. The exporter might have to lower their prices and eat the cost of the tariff to keep selling to the United States. […]

On the other hand, a country that is the main source of a particular good has a lot more leverage. […]

Many Chinese goods fall into this category because China dominates the global market for many products Americans buy. Even when faced with a 10 percent tariff, American importers will have to continue buying products such as laptops and smartphones from China because no other country can produce the same volume for American consumers.

Sometimes a country with a relatively small slice of the export market can keep its grip on American markets because it has the infrastructure to deliver the goods. If a tariff is applied to Canada, for instance, Canadian exporters of crude oil and natural gas won't need to reduce their prices because American pipelines and refineries are set up specifically for Canadian fossil fuels.

Some products are more complex, with supply chains crossing borders several times, so tariffs pile up with additional costs. A vehicle assembled in Mexico can have parts from the United States and Canada. Tariffs might hurt the Mexican businesses that assemble the vehicles, but they will also hurt American factories that supply the parts.

Broad tariffs for close trade partners or essential goods can have ripple effects across the entire economy. If a company has to pay more for its imports because of a tariff, it might need to raise prices or lay off workers to stay in business. […]

The negative effects can be magnified if countries retaliate with tariffs of their own, potentially reducing the demand for American goods abroad. After China imposed retaliatory tariffs on American goods in 2018, American exports to China dropped by 26 percent.

Last week, China announced counter-tariffs against the United States just minutes after the Trump administration announced its 10 percent levy on Chinese goods. […]

Source: The Washington Post, "Who pays for Trump's tariffs? Depends on the product and the country." — Alyssa Fowers and Luis Melgar, 11 February 2025.

05 Build a Strong Argument

Un argument oral efficace suit quatre temps : IDEA → EXPLAIN → EXAMPLE → LINK.

Why this matters
Idea only — too weakTariffs make imported goods more expensive.
+ Explain — betterTariffs make imported goods more expensive, but who actually pays that extra cost depends on whether the exporting country can be easily replaced.
+ Example — stronger…For example, garment exporters often have to lower their own prices to keep selling to the US, because importers can simply switch to another supplier.
Part 1 — Direct cost effects Build one argument

Modèle — Part 1

Idea — A tariff does not automatically make the exporting country pay — the cost often shifts depending on who has market power.

Explain — Because when a good can easily be sourced elsewhere, importers can simply switch suppliers, forcing the exporter to absorb the cost; but when a country dominates supply, importers have no alternative.

Example — For example, China's dominance in laptops and smartphones means a 10 percent tariff is largely paid by American consumers, since importers keep buying from China anyway.

Link — This shows that the direct cost of a tariff is not fixed — it depends on which side of the trade relationship holds more leverage.

Part 2 — Structural effects on sourcing Build one argument

Modèle — Part 2

Idea — Tariffs push companies to redesign their supply chains, not just accept higher costs.

Explain — Because with multi-stage production, a tariff on one country can hurt several economies linked in the same chain, so firms diversify sourcing to reduce their exposure.

Example — For example, a vehicle assembled in Mexico with US and Canadian parts means a tariff on Mexico also damages American parts suppliers, pushing manufacturers to reconsider where they locate production.

Link — This shows that tariffs can reshape entire production networks, not just prices, as firms try to insulate themselves from future trade disputes.

08 Nuance Your Answer

Une question sur un mécanisme n'accepte jamais une réponse tout-ou-rien. Utilisez may, can, could, to some extent, depending on, unless…

Too binaryTariffs make supply chains collapse.
NuancedTariffs can seriously disrupt supply chains, but the extent of the disruption depends on how easily a product can be resourced and whether the exporting country holds real market leverage.
Rewrite this claim with more nuance:« Tariffs always force companies to move their production out of the targeted country. » Utilisez au moins un mot de nuance.

Modèle — nuance

Tariffs can push companies to relocate part of their production, but this largely depends on whether the targeted country has unique advantages, such as infrastructure or a dominant market share, that keep it hard to replace.

Introduction

Tariffs affect global supply chains in ways that go well beyond a simple price increase, and the severity of the effect can vary a great deal. They clearly raise costs somewhere in the chain, but who pays and how deeply the chain is restructured depends on the product and the country involved. I will first consider their direct cost effects before examining how they reshape sourcing decisions and their broader ripple effects.

Part 1. Direct cost effects
  • Economists warn that the added tariff cost will largely fall on American businesses and consumers.Source : texte
  • A country is more vulnerable when its goods can be sourced elsewhere and the US is its main buyer, since importers can simply switch suppliers.Source : texte
  • By contrast, a country that dominates global production, such as China with laptops and smartphones, keeps leverage and does not need to lower its prices.Source : texte
  • Higher import costs are often passed on to retail prices, feeding inflation in the importing country.Source : connaissances
Part 2. Structural effects on sourcing
  • Multi-stage supply chains mean tariffs pile up: a vehicle assembled in Mexico can contain parts from the US and Canada.Source : texte
  • A tariff on one country in such a chain can therefore hurt factories in several countries at once.Source : texte
  • Companies respond by diversifying suppliers — for example, Apple shifting some production toward Vietnam and India to reduce its exposure to any single country.Source : connaissances
  • Infrastructure can also lock in a supplier despite tariffs, as with Canadian oil and gas moving through US pipelines built specifically for it.Source : texte
Part 3. Broader ripple effects & overall assessment
  • Broad tariffs on close trade partners or essential goods can ripple through the whole economy, raising prices or costing jobs.Source : texte
  • Retaliatory tariffs can cut demand for exports — American exports to China fell 26 percent after China's 2018 retaliation.Source : texte
  • Multilateral rules, such as those of the WTO, exist precisely to prevent this kind of escalating tariff war.Source : connaissances
  • Overall, the severity of the disruption depends less on the tariff rate itself than on how substitutable the product is and who holds market leverage.Source : connaissances
Conclusion

Overall, tariffs affect global supply chains on several levels at once: they shift costs toward whichever side has less market power, they push companies to redesign or diversify their sourcing, and they can trigger economy-wide ripple effects through retaliation. The depth of the disruption is never uniform — it depends on how easily a product can be replaced and how tightly production networks are already integrated across borders.
