Source Document

Many retailers offer 'returnless refunds'. Just don't expect them to say for which products.

Haleluya Hadero, The Associated Press, November 4, 2024

It's one of the most under-publicized policies of some of the biggest U.S. retailers: sometimes they give customers full refunds and let them keep unwanted items too. Returnless refunds are a tool that more retailers are using to keep online shoppers happy and to reduce shipping fees, processing time and other ballooning costs from returned products.

Companies such as Amazon, Walmart and Target have decided some items are not worth the cost or hassle of getting back. Think a $20 T-shirt that might cost $30 in shipping and handling to recover. There are also single-use items, such as a package of plastic straws, that might be difficult to resell or medicines that could be unsafe to market again. […]

While the retail practice of letting customers keep merchandise and get their money back is not exactly a trade secret, the way it works is shrouded in mystery. Companies are not keen to publicize the circumstances in which they issue returnless refunds due to concerns over the potential for return fraud.

Even if brands don't provide details about such policies on their websites, returnless refunds are expanding in at least some retail corners. Amazon, which industry experts say has engaged in the practice for years, announced in August that it would extend the option to the third-party sellers who drive most of the sales on the e-commerce giant's platform. […]

Overall, retailers and brands tend to be careful about how often they let customers keep items for free. Many of them are deploying algorithms to determine who should be given the option and who should not. To make the decision, the algorithms assess multiple factors, including the extent to which a shopper should be trusted based on prior purchasing — and returning — patterns, shipping costs and the demand for the product in the customer's hands, according to Sender Shamiss, CEO of goTRG, a reverse logistics company that works with retailers like Walmart. […]

Some retailers also are stiffening the liberal return policies they long employed to encourage online orders. Shoppers who enjoyed making purchases on their computers or cellphones became accustomed to loading up their digital shopping baskets with the intent of returning items they ended up not liking.

Shopping online also grew significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic, when homebound consumers reduced their trips to stores and relied on sites like Amazon for everyday items. Retail companies have talked in recent years about returns becoming more expensive to process due to the growing volume, rising inflation and labor costs. […]

Last year, roughly 14% of returns were fraudulent, costing retailers $101 billion in losses, according to a joint report from the National Retail Federation and Appriss Retail. The problem spans from low-level forms of fraud — such as shoppers returning already worn clothing — to more complicated schemes by fraudsters who return shoplifted merchandise or items purchased on stolen credit cards.

To deter excessive returns, some retailers, including H&M, Zara and J. Crew, started charging customers return fees in the past year. Others have shortened their return windows. Some shopping sites, such as the Canadian retailer Ssense, have threatened to kick frequent returners off their platforms if they suspect abuse of their policies.

However, retailers don't all view frequent returners in the same way. Such customers could be seen as "good returners" if they purchase — and keep — many more items than they send back, Ali said. "Oftentimes, your most profitable customers tend to be high returners," she said.

Step 1 / 7

Understand

Guiding question: To what extent can new purchasing trends have an impact on logistics and marketing strategies? Before you can summarise a text, separate what it is about from what the author thinks about it.

Topic

The subject the text discusses. Neutral — it doesn't tell you the author's opinion.

Author's position

What the author actually argues or concludes about that topic.

TOPIC ≠ AUTHOR'S POSITION — a good summary always states both, separately.
What is the TOPIC of this article?
What is the AUTHOR'S POSITION on this topic?
Step 2 / 7

Select

Not every sentence deserves a place in your summary. Learn to recognise a main idea — and tell it apart from an example, a statistic, or a claim that is too vague to be useful.

2.1 — Spot the main idea

This passage from the text can be rewritten four ways. Which formulation actually states its main idea?

Same task, a different passage.
"...the algorithms assess multiple factors, including the extent to which a shopper should be trusted based on prior purchasing — and returning — patterns, shipping costs and the demand for the product in the customer's hands..."

2.2 — Keep or leave out?

For each statement drawn from the article, decide: does it belong in a summary?

MAIN IDEAS → keep. EXAMPLES, FIGURES and SECONDARY SOURCES → usually leave out.

Step 4 / 7

Reformulate

Four reusable techniques to put ideas into your own words — the skill you'll need for every future summary, not just this one.

4.1 — Synonym

Replace ballooning with a suitable synonym and rewrite the sentence.
"Returnless refunds help retailers reduce ballooning shipping and processing costs."
Model — Returnless refunds help retailers reduce rising shipping and processing costs.

4.2 — Change the structure

Rewrite this sentence in the passive voice.
"Retailers deploy algorithms to determine who gets a returnless refund."
Model — Algorithms are deployed by retailers to determine who gets a returnless refund.

4.3 — Generalise

These three retailers share something in common. Name the category in a few words.
H&M · Zara · J. Crew
Model — clothing retailers

4.4 — Combine

Merge these three short sentences into one.
"Some retailers charge return fees. Some retailers shorten return windows. Some retailers threaten to ban frequent returners."
Model — Some retailers charge return fees, shorten return windows or threaten to ban frequent returners.

Model answer

The article examines how retailers are managing the rising cost of online returns. The author explains that many retailers now offer returnless refunds, letting customers keep unwanted items and get their money back when the cost of shipping or reselling the product outweighs its value. To decide who qualifies, retailers such as Amazon and Walmart use algorithms that weigh a shopper's trust based on past behaviour, shipping costs and product demand. At the same time, growing return volumes and processing costs, driven partly by online shopping habits formed during the pandemic, are pushing many retailers to tighten policies that were once more liberal, for instance by charging return fees or shortening return windows. This is partly a response to fraud: returning worn clothing or exploiting stolen cards costs retailers over $100 billion a year. However, the article concludes that frequent returners are not necessarily unprofitable, since many buy far more than they send back.

Summary Method

  1. Understand

    What is the text about? What is the author's position?

  2. Select

    Keep main ideas. Remove unnecessary evidence.

  3. Condense

    Combine several details into one broader idea.

  4. Reformulate

    Use your own words and structures.

  5. Organise

    Build a logical plan.

  6. Write

    Be concise, accurate and neutral.

  7. Check

    Content, selection, reformulation, organisation, language and word count.
