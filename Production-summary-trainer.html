Who pays for Trump's tariffs? Depends on the product and the country.

Alyssa Fowers and Luis Melgar, The Washington Post, February 11, 2025

Since taking office, President Donald Trump has threatened tariffs on an array of trading partners, including Mexico, Canada, Europe, Colombia and India. He has instituted a 10 percent tariff on Chinese goods and a 25 percent tariff on aluminum and steel imports.

Which nation ultimately bears the cost of a tariff depends on what that country makes and its pull in the global market. But economists warn that the added tax will largely fall on American businesses and consumers.

The United States is the largest importer of goods in the world, giving it significant sway in a trade war. It is also the top export market for more than 20 countries, making exports to the United States a sizable portion of many countries' economies.

Mexico's and Canada's economies are the most reliant on exports to the United States; both countries were threatened with 25 percent tariffs that are now under a 30-day reprieve. But that doesn't mean they are necessarily the most vulnerable to tariffs. Who bears the cost and risk of the trade tax depends more on the product than where it is made.

A country is most vulnerable if it primarily produces goods that can easily be made in many places, such as garments or agricultural products, and the United States is its biggest customer. In those cases, American importers could substitute goods from another country to avoid paying the tariff. The exporter might have to lower their prices and eat the cost of the tariff to keep selling to the United States. […]

On the other hand, a country that is the main source of a particular good has a lot more leverage. […]

Many Chinese goods fall into this category because China dominates the global market for many products Americans buy. Even when faced with a 10 percent tariff, American importers will have to continue buying products such as laptops and smartphones from China because no other country can produce the same volume for American consumers.

Sometimes a country with a relatively small slice of the export market can keep its grip on American markets because it has the infrastructure to deliver the goods. If a tariff is applied to Canada, for instance, Canadian exporters of crude oil and natural gas won't need to reduce their prices because American pipelines and refineries are set up specifically for Canadian fossil fuels.

Some products are more complex, with supply chains crossing borders several times, so tariffs pile up with additional costs. A vehicle assembled in Mexico can have parts from the United States and Canada. Tariffs might hurt the Mexican businesses that assemble the vehicles, but they will also hurt American factories that supply the parts.

Broad tariffs for close trade partners or essential goods can have ripple effects across the entire economy. If a company has to pay more for its imports because of a tariff, it might need to raise prices or lay off workers to stay in business. […]

The negative effects can be magnified if countries retaliate with tariffs of their own, potentially reducing the demand for American goods abroad. After China imposed retaliatory tariffs on American goods in 2018, American exports to China dropped by 26 percent.

Last week, China announced counter-tariffs against the United States just minutes after the Trump administration announced its 10 percent levy on Chinese goods. […]

Source: The Washington Post, "Who pays for Trump's tariffs? Depends on the product and the country." — Alyssa Fowers and Luis Melgar, 11 February 2025.

Step 1 / 7

Understand

Guiding question: How do tariffs affect global supply chains? Before you can summarise a text, separate what it is about from what the author thinks about it.

Topic

The subject the text discusses. Neutral — it doesn't tell you the author's opinion.

Author's position

What the author actually argues or concludes about that topic.

TOPIC ≠ AUTHOR'S POSITION — a good summary always states both, separately.

Step 2 / 7

Select

Not every sentence deserves a place in your summary. Learn to recognise a main idea — and tell it apart from an example, a statistic, or a claim that is too vague to be useful.

Same task, a different passage.

3.3 — Find the right level of detail

Match each candidate sentence about Canada's oil and gas exports to the right label.

"US pipelines built for Canadian crude mean Canada won't need to cut oil and gas prices even under a 25% tariff."
"Canada keeps pricing power over oil and gas because US infrastructure is built specifically for it."
"Canada's oil and gas industry can therefore absorb a 25% tariff without cutting export prices to its main customer, the United States."
Step 4 / 7

Reformulate

Four reusable techniques to put ideas into your own words — the skill you'll need for every future summary, not just this one.

4.1 — Synonym

Replace bears with a suitable synonym and rewrite the sentence.
"Economists warn that American businesses and consumers will bear the added cost of tariffs."
Model — Economists warn that American businesses and consumers will absorb the added cost of tariffs.

4.2 — Change the structure

Rewrite this sentence in the passive voice.
"China imposed retaliatory tariffs on American goods in 2018."
Model — Retaliatory tariffs were imposed on American goods by China in 2018.

4.3 — Generalise

These three things share something in common. Name the category in a few words.
garments · agricultural products · furniture
Model — easily substitutable goods

4.4 — Combine

Merge these three short sentences into one.
"Tariffs can raise prices. Tariffs can cost jobs. Tariffs can trigger retaliation."
Model — Tariffs can raise prices, cost jobs and trigger retaliation.

Model answer

The article examines how the cost of Donald Trump's tariffs is shared between the United States and its trading partners. The author argues that, although the US has significant leverage as the world's largest importer, economists expect the added cost to fall mainly on American businesses and consumers. This depends more on the product than on the exporting country: a country is vulnerable when its goods can easily be sourced elsewhere and the US is its main buyer, because importers can simply switch suppliers, forcing exporters to absorb the tariff. However, a country that dominates global production of a good, such as China with laptops and smartphones, keeps more leverage and does not need to lower its prices. Tariffs on close trading partners can also have wider effects, raising prices or costing jobs across supply chains. Finally, the article concludes that retaliatory tariffs risk reducing demand for American exports abroad, as happened when US exports to China fell after the 2018 trade war.

Summary Method

  1. Understand

    What is the text about? What is the author's position?

  2. Select

    Keep main ideas. Remove unnecessary evidence.

  3. Condense

    Combine several details into one broader idea.

  4. Reformulate

    Use your own words and structures.

  5. Organise

    Build a logical plan.

  6. Write

    Be concise, accurate and neutral.

  7. Check

    Content, selection, reformulation, organisation, language and word count.
